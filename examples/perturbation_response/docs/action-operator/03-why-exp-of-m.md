# 3. Why $\exp(M)$

*Chapter 2 argued the latent operator should be linear, which leaves $z' = Az$ for some matrix $A$. So learn $A$. Why route it through a second matrix and a matrix exponential? The answer is that $\exp(M)$ is not a parameterization of the operator, it is a statement about where the operator came from, and once you accept that statement a surprising amount stops being a design decision and starts being a consequence.*

> **Where this sits.** [Chapter 1](01-what-an-operator-is.md) gave the operator its meaning (the commuting square). [Chapter 2](02-why-linear-koopman.md) gave it its linear form (Koopman coordinates). This chapter gives it its *shape*, and it is also where the near-identity prior, invertibility, and clean composition all stop being separate wishes and become one fact with three faces.

---

## 1. The obvious thing, and four reasons it is wrong

The obvious construction is to let the network emit $A$ directly: $D^2$ numbers, arranged into a matrix, applied to $z$. It is simple, it is expressive, and it fails in four ways that matter.

**It can be singular.** Nothing stops a freely emitted $A$ from having determinant zero, or near zero. A singular operator collapses directions of latent space irreversibly: two distinct cells map to the same outcome and the transformation cannot be undone. If you believe an intervention transforms a system rather than destroying information about it, you want invertibility, and here you would have to *enforce* it.

**"Do nothing" is not at the origin.** The identity is the single most important operator in the framework, since Chapter 1's anchor says an effect is a departure from it. In a free parameterization the identity sits at an arbitrary point $A = I$, which you must initialize to deliberately, and toward which no natural regularizer pulls. Shrinking the parameters toward zero drives $A \to 0$, the *annihilation* operator, which is the worst possible map.

**The space has a hole.** Invertible matrices form $GL(D)$, which is **not a vector space**. It is a manifold with the singular matrices removed. A network's output layer emits points in a flat space, and asking it to emit points on a curved space with a hole in it is asking for trouble: gradient steps do not respect the manifold, and interpolating two valid operators can pass straight through the hole.

**Composition and repetition are numerically awkward.** Applying the same operator $k$ times means $A^k$, computed by $k$ multiplications, and the result is generically either exploding or vanishing. There is no cheap way to ask "what does a hundred steps do" and no meaningful way to ask for "half a step."

Each of these could be patched. The point of what follows is that none of them needs patching, because they are all symptoms of modeling the *destination* instead of the *motion*.

## 2. The answer: $A$ is the time-one map of a flow

Stop asking for a matrix that maps here to there. Ask instead for the **velocity field** that carries one to the other.

Let the state move continuously, with a velocity that is linear in the state:

$$\dot{z} = M z .$$

Read $M$ as saying "at the point $z$, move in direction $Mz$." This is a linear autonomous ordinary differential equation, and it has a closed-form solution:

$$z(t) = \exp(tM) z(0), \qquad \exp(tM) = I + tM + \tfrac{1}{2}(tM)^2 + \tfrac{1}{6}(tM)^3 + \cdots$$

Evaluate at $t = 1$ and you have the operator:

$$A = \exp(M).$$

So $M$ and $A$ are not two parameterizations of one thing. They are **two different objects**: $M$ is the *generator*, the instantaneous tendency, and $A$ is the *evolution*, where that tendency takes you after one unit of time. The exponential is the map from one to the other, and it is not a modeling choice, it is the solution of the differential equation.

This is exactly the object Chapter 2 arrived at from the other direction. There the Koopman family $\mathcal{K}_t$ satisfied $\mathcal{K}_{t+u} = \mathcal{K}_t\mathcal{K}_u$ with $\mathcal{K}_0 = I$, and such a family is generated by a single operator $L$ with $\mathcal{K}_t = \exp(tL)$. Restricted to a finite invariant subspace, $L$ is our $M$. **The flow story and the Koopman story are the same story.**

> **The affine case, so it is not left dangling.** Chapter 1 wrote the operator as $f_\theta(z) = A_\theta z + b_\theta$. Add a constant forcing term to the velocity, $\dot{z} = Mz + b$, and integrating gives $z(1) = \exp(M) z_0 + M^{-1}(\exp(M) - I) b$. The affine operator is the flow of an affine velocity field, bias and all. The classical "add a learned perturbation vector" model of an intervention is the further special case $M = 0$, which is a pure translation with no reshaping at all.

## 3. Why this is structural rather than convenient

It is worth being explicit about the strength of the claim, because "we parameterize $A$ as $\exp(M)$ for numerical reasons" is a much weaker statement than the one on offer.

A family of maps indexed by time that satisfies

$$\Phi_{t+u} = \Phi_t \Phi_u, \qquad \Phi_0 = I$$

is called a **one-parameter semigroup**. The two conditions say only what any notion of "evolving for a while" must say: evolving for $t$ then for $u$ is evolving for $t + u$, and evolving for no time does nothing. And the generator theory of such families says that every one of them, under mild continuity, is $\Phi_t = \exp(tM)$ for exactly one $M$.

> **The claim.** $\exp$ is not a way of *representing* an operator. It is what an operator **is**, whenever that operator came from something evolving. Writing $A = \exp(M)$ asserts that a flow produced $A$, and names its generator.

That assertion is what the rest of the chapter cashes in, and it is also what can be false. An operator that did not come from a flow is not obliged to be an exponential, and §8 is about what happens when you write $\exp$ anyway and never use what it bought.

## 4. What falls out for free

Every problem in §1 dissolves, and none of the fixes is a fix. They are consequences.

| property | why it holds | what it buys |
|---|---|---|
| **always invertible** | $\exp(M)\exp(-M) = \exp(0) = I$, for *any* $M$ | undoable dynamics, no constraint to enforce, and $M \mapsto -M$ runs time backwards |
| **identity at the origin** | $\exp(0) = I$ | "this intervention does nothing" is $M = 0$, so a zero-initialized network starts at the identity and shrinking the parameters pulls toward it |
| **flat parameterization** | $M$ ranges over all matrices, a vector space | a network can emit $M$ gracefully; it could not emit a point on a curved manifold with a hole |
| **repetition collapses** | $\exp(M)^k = \exp(kM)$ | $k$ steps in one matrix, and $k$ can even be fractional |
| **interpolation is meaningful** | $\exp(tM)$ for $t \in [0,1]$ | a continuous path from $I$ to $A$, so "half the intervention" is defined rather than invented |
| **the spectrum is readable** | $\mathrm{eig}(\exp(M)) = \exp(\mathrm{eig}(M))$ | an eigenvalue $a + bi$ of $M$ gives growth or decay at rate $e^{a}$ and rotation by angle $b$: what the operator amplifies, damps, and rotates, read off directly |
| **magnitude is meaningful** | $\lVert M \rVert$ measures the velocity field | a natural least-action penalty, and the bound of §6 |

Two of these deserve a second look.

**The identity at the origin is not a small convenience.** It aligns three things that would otherwise have to be coordinated by hand: the *initialization* (zero-init the last layer and every operator starts as a no-op), the *regularizer* (weight decay on $M$ pulls toward doing nothing rather than toward annihilation), and the *semantics* (Chapter 1's anchor, that an effect is a departure from the identity). In a free parameterization these three pull in three directions.

**The spectrum turns the operator into something you can read.** A skew-symmetric $M$ (meaning $M^\top = -M$) has purely imaginary eigenvalues, so $\exp(M)$ is a pure rotation that changes no lengths. A symmetric $M$ has real eigenvalues, so $\exp(M)$ is a pure stretch along fixed axes with no rotation. A general $M$ mixes the two. You can therefore look at a learned intervention and say what it does, which a free matrix affords much less cleanly.

## 5. Structure by construction: the Lie picture

The framing that organizes all of this, and the one GRL's formalization reaches for, is that $M$ and $A$ live in different mathematical objects.

The invertible matrices form a **Lie group** $G$: a set that is both a group (composition, inverses, identity) and a smooth manifold. Its **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{g}$ is the tangent space at the identity, the space of "infinitesimal motions away from doing nothing," and it *is* a vector space. The exponential map sends one to the other:

$$\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G .$$

That is the whole design in one line: **the network works in the flat algebra, and the exponential delivers a valid group element.** GRL states the motivation exactly this way, that the policy can output a Lie algebra element, which lives in a vector space and is easy to parameterize, and exponentiation produces the operator.

The payoff is that **constraining $M$ constrains $A$ to a subgroup**, automatically:

| constrain $M$ to | $\exp(M)$ lands in | the operator is |
|---|---|---|
| all matrices | $GL^{+}(D)$ | any invertible, orientation-preserving map |
| skew-symmetric, $M^\top = -M$ | $SO(D)$ | a rotation: no lengths change |
| symmetric | positive-definite matrices | a pure stretch: no rotation |
| $\mathfrak{se}(3)$, six generators | $SE(3)$ | a rigid motion in space |

This is how domain knowledge enters the model as *structure* rather than as a penalty the optimizer may ignore. If you know an intervention cannot change the scale of the state, make $M$ skew and the model **cannot** represent a scaling, no matter what the data suggests.

> **A note on provenance.** GRL proposes this parameterization in its formalization as the natural way to give a policy a linear handle on a nonlinear operator space, and lists the Hamiltonian form $\exp(\tau J \nabla H)$ as planned. It was never implemented there; there is no matrix exponential anywhere in that repository. The module accompanying this series is, as far as we know, the first working instance of GRL's own idea, which is a pleasant thing for an application to hand back to the theory it borrowed from.

## 6. Least action, and the reason it matters twice

The Frobenius penalty $\lVert M \rVert_F^2$ is usually introduced as an inductive bias: interventions are small structured changes, so keep the generator small. That is the first reason and it is the weaker one.

The second reason is that **least action is what makes composed operators predictable**. Because the operator norm is submultiplicative and $\lVert \exp(M) \rVert \le \exp(\lVert M \rVert)$, a sequence of operators satisfies

$$\big\lVert \exp(M_K) \cdots \exp(M_1) \big\rVert \le \exp\Big( \sum_i \lVert M_i \rVert \Big).$$

So the total displacement a whole plan can produce is bounded, *before you run it*, by the sum of its generators' norms. Small generators compose into a bounded whole. This is the same shape as the subadditive energy bound in GRL's formalization, and it is what makes it possible to reason about a plan as an object rather than by simulation.

The catch is that the bound is exponential in $\lVert M \rVert$, so it is informative only when the generators are genuinely small and vacuous otherwise. A median generator norm of $12$ gives a bound around $2 \times 10^{5}$, which is true and useless.

> **A consequence worth carrying.** The least-action penalty and the planning horizon are coupled. Weaken the penalty for fidelity and the bounds on composed behavior go vacuous; strengthen it for bounds and the operator may no longer fit the data. That trade is not hypothetical: Chapter 7 reports it as a measured conflict.

## 7. What $\exp$ does not give you

Four honest limits, none fatal, all worth knowing.

**It cannot reach every invertible matrix.** For real $M$, Jacobi's formula gives $\det \exp(M) = \exp(\mathrm{tr}\ M) > 0$ always. So the image is only the **identity component** $GL^{+}(D)$, and an orientation-reversing map (a reflection) is unreachable. For dynamics that evolve continuously from doing nothing this is exactly right, since you cannot reach a reflection by flowing, but it is a genuine restriction rather than a free lunch.

**It is not injective.** Distinct generators can give the same operator: a rotation by $2\pi$ and by $0$ are both the identity. So "the generator of this operator" is well defined only locally, near $M = 0$, which is one more reason the near-identity regime is where the framework is on solid ground.

**It costs something.** A matrix exponential is roughly $O(D^3)$ by scaling and squaring, and its gradient is more expensive still. This is why our implementation computes **one exponential per intervention rather than per sample**: the operator is a property of the intervention, not of the state it acts on, which makes the cost negligible instead of prohibitive.

**The logarithm is only well behaved near the identity.** Recovering $M$ from $A$, which the composition algebra of Chapter 5 needs, requires $\log$, and $\log$ is single-valued only in a neighborhood of $I$. Far from it, the correspondence between generators and operators frays. This is the same boundary that will make or break the bracket story.

## 8. The premise, and what its silent failure looks like

Every chapter here states a premise and the shape of its failure. This one's premise is:

> $A = \exp(M)$ because a flow produced $A$, and $M$ is that flow's generator.

The failure mode is subtle, because it produces no error and no bad number. It is this: **you can write $\exp(M)$ and never use any of it.**

Apply the exponential once, to one generator, to produce one operator, and then never compose two of them, never interpolate along $\exp(tM)$, never read the spectrum, never exploit $\exp(M)^k = \exp(kM)$. In that case $\exp$ has bought you exactly two things, invertibility and a convenient near-identity initialization, and it has cost you a matrix exponential per step. Everything else in §4 was available and unused. The model is a linear map with a good initialization, wearing the notation of a flow.

That is not hypothetical. It is precisely what the first operator round in this project did, and the diagnosis is in the [results ledger](../conditional-flow-jepa/results-ledger.md): the matrix exponential was doing the work of a **reparameterization** and never the work of a **capability**. The round tied the free flow it was meant to replace, which is the arithmetic you should expect when a restriction removes capacity and a prior adds bias in equal measure.

The lesson generalizes past this project, and it is the third row of the [premise table](index.md): **if you adopt $\exp(M)$, use the algebra it gives you.** If the model never composes, never interpolates, and never inspects, then a directly parameterized matrix with an identity initialization and a spectral penalty would have done the same work more cheaply, and the framework is decoration.
